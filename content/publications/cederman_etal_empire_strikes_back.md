# The Empire Strikes Back: Imperial Loss, Dominant Nationalism, and Interstate Conflict

**When does territorial loss become a source of interstate conflict? In our new working paper, we focus on lost imperial territory, arguing that past losses can become dangerous when dominant nationalists reinterpret them as national humiliation or threats and turn former imperial space into a program of restoration. Using historical spatial data reaching back to 1100, we find that imperial loss alone does not reliably predict conflict—but that it becomes strongly conflict-prone once dominant nationalists govern the potential challenger.**

Empires are often treated as remnants of a bygone era. Yet recent crises, most visibly Russia's war against Ukraine, but also China's claims over Taiwan and renewed debates over U.S. hemispheric influence, suggest that older maps and memories of lost greatness continue to shape geopolitics. Not all territorial losses produce revisionism: some are accepted, forgotten, or commemorated in non-violent ways. We ask when territorial loss becomes imperial loss, and when such claims escalate into interstate conflict.

A large literature offers three partial answers. Work on ethnic configurations after empire shows how borders strand co-nationals and link host states to external homelands. Work on restorative nationalism adds a temporal dimension, showing how nationalists frame present borders as the result of lost unity. Work on imperial legacies shows that empires and nation-states are not cleanly sequential. But this last literature often leaves underspecified who mobilizes imperial legacies and whether conflict is driven by the recovery of co-nationals or by the restoration of a broader former order.

We address this ambiguity by distinguishing two restorative claims. Lost-kin claims target co-nationals and divided homelands. Lost-empire claims target territories associated with a predecessor polity, even where co-national settlement is weak or absent. The first restores the nation; the second restores a former structure of rule. Dominant nationalists—who claim political primacy for one nation and project this hierarchy outward—supply the actor and the ideological trigger that make such claims consequential.

To test the argument, we combine new spatial measures of historical territorial loss with data on dominant nationalist rule, drawn from the Dominant Nationalist Movements database, and interstate conflict in directed dyad-years. We first analyze European territorial histories back to 1100, then extend to a global sample from 1886, using two-stage difference-in-differences estimators.

The results show that imperial loss on its own does not reliably predict conflict. Yet when dominant nationalists govern the potential challenger, past loss and current rule together raise the annual risk of hostilities by almost 6%. Disaggregating the loss measure shows this is not reducible to standard irredentism: former imperial territory without co-national settlement also becomes conflict-prone under dominant nationalist rule. The risk is greatest where lost territory harbors co-nationals living as a subordinate minority—the constellation in which lost-kin and lost-empire claims coincide, as in Russia and Ukraine.

We illustrate the mechanism in six cases—Russia, Germany, Serbia, Hungary, China, and Turkey—tracing how dominant nationalists politicized the contraction of a predecessor polity and, in several instances, translated it into interstate conflict. Lost empire supplies the map; dominant nationalism turns it into a mission.
