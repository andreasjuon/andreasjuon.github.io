# Dominant Nationalism:  State Power, Imperialism, and the Return of War

# Dominant Nationalism: State Power, Imperialism, and the Return of War

**Interstate conflict has reached levels not seen since the Second World War, while civil wars have resurged at the same time. In this book, written together with Lars-Erik Cederman, I tie this return of war to the revival of dominant nationalism: political movements that claim the state as the property of a single nation and seek political dominance over others.**

In March 2014, Vladimir Putin justified Russia's annexation of Crimea by invoking the historic greatness and unity of the Russian nation, setting in motion the most devastating war in Europe since 1945. This is part of a broader, simultaneous resurgence of warfare and dominant nationalism, particularly affecting some of the world's most militarily powerful states. Internationally, dominant nationalist-ruled states have pursued territorial revisionism, regional hegemony, and conquest: Russia invaded Ukraine, China has escalated its claims over Taiwan, Israel and Iran are engaged in a struggle for regional primacy, and the United States has revived the language of the Monroe Doctrine. Domestically, the same governments have intensified exclusionary politics, fueling civil conflicts driven by the grievances of marginalized and repressed groups, as in Myanmar, Israel/Palestine, and Sudan.

Existing research linking nationalism to armed conflict is built around the principle of nation-state congruence: the idea that political and national units should coincide. This perspective explains secessionism, irredentism, stranded minorities, and external homelands. However, it leaves three blind spots. First, it treats nationalism and empire as mutually exclusive, so nationalist projects that seek to dominate outgroups or expand beyond existing borders into territories inhabited by non-nationals appear non-nationalist. Second, it assumes that empire belongs to the past, making contemporary imperial politics look like an anomaly and complicating efforts to study causes and potential remedies. Third, it treats nationalism as a structural outcome of industrialization and state-building rather than as the political project of specific actors, which leaves open who advances claims to dominance, and when.

Addressing these blind spots, we redefine nationalism as a political ideology that claims state power for a nation and, typically, its unity within that state. Congruence is no longer part of the definition, but we retain it as a classificatory device: nationalist claims may seek congruence, undershoot it by accepting a shared state, or overshoot it. The book focuses on the latter, distinguishing two variants thereof — dominant nationalism, in which one nation claims the right to rule others within the existing state, and its imperialist subtype, which extends that claim beyond existing borders. Prominent contemporary examples include Hindu nationalists in India, white nationalists in the United States, Han nationalists in China, and Russian nationalists in Russia.

To trace the causes and consequences of these projects across two centuries, we draw on the new Dominant Nationalist Movements dataset, which covers nationalist movements and governments worldwide from 1816 to 2023, and adds time-varying ideological indicators for a representative subsample of 90 multi-ethnic states since 1946. This allows us to identify dominant and imperialist nationalist actors directly, rather than inferring them from patterns of exclusion or ethnic geography.

Combining these data with statistical analyses and comparative case studies, we show that dominant nationalism systematically increases the risk of both civil and interstate war. It does so by fostering exclusion, repression, territorial revisionism, and historical claims to lost empires. We also find that dominant nationalist governments are more likely both to initiate interstate conflict and to become targets of attack, especially where their ideology crosses the threshold to open imperialism.

We further examine how dominant nationalism can be contained. We find little evidence that appeasement reduces conflict. Instead, dominant nationalism tends to recede where its hierarchical ambitions are frustrated and excluded groups achieve durable political inclusion. Conversely, where dominant nationalist-ruled states are allowed to expand and amass material power, they become increasingly vulnerable to collapse — through state failure, foreign intervention, civil war, and reactive nationalism abroad — as their imperial ambitions overstretch.
